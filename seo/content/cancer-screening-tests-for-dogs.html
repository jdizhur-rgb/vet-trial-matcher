Blood and urine tests are increasingly offered as a way to find cancer before a dog develops symptoms. But “a cancer test” can mean several different things: looking for a particular mutation, measuring material released by damaged cells, or detecting an immune response associated with a tumor.

Some tests look for specific cancers. Others flag a broader possibility that needs investigation. Their usefulness depends on what they measure, which cancers they can detect and what happens after the result.

This review focuses on testing apparently healthy dogs. Monitoring a known cancer and assessing a lump that has already been found are different tasks; results from those settings cannot automatically establish how well a test works for preventive screening.

How to read sensitivity and specificity

A test can make two basic mistakes: miss cancer that is present, or raise an alarm when the cancer it is looking for is absent.

Sensitivity tells us how many dogs with the disease the test detects. If 100 dogs have confirmed cancer and 80 test positive, sensitivity in that group is 80%. The other 20 have false-negative results: the disease is present, but the test missed it.

Sensitivity = true positives ÷ all dogs with the disease × 100%.

Specificity tells us how many dogs without the disease the test correctly identifies as negative. If 990 of 1,000 dogs without the target disease test negative, specificity is 99%. The other 10 have false-positive results.

Specificity = true negatives ÷ all dogs without the disease × 100%.

These numbers describe performance in a particular study population. They can change with the cancer types, stages and other illnesses represented. A result from dogs with established diagnoses is not necessarily the result to expect when screening apparently healthy senior dogs.

Why 99% specificity does not mean a 99% chance of cancer

Suppose 10,000 dogs are screened. The target cancer is present in 1%, or 100 dogs. Our hypothetical test has 80% sensitivity and 99% specificity.

Cancer present: 100 dogs80 correctly detected20 missed
Cancer absent: 9,900 dogs99 false alarms9,801 correctly negative

There are 179 positive results, but only 80 represent cancer: 80 ÷ 179 ≈ 45%. This is the positive predictive value: the proportion of positive results that are true positives.

Use the same test in a group where 10% have the target disease, and that proportion rises to about 90%: 800 true positives divided by 800 true positives plus 90 false positives. The test has not changed; the underlying risk has.

This is why a positive result needs to be interpreted alongside the dog's age, breed, examination and other findings. Equally, a negative result is not a guarantee that no cancer is present.

Nu.Q Vet: nucleosomes in blood

Nucleosomes are small packages of DNA wrapped around proteins called histones. They can enter the bloodstream when cells die. Nu.Q measures circulating nucleosomes containing histone H3.1.

It does not search for a particular cancer mutation. An elevated result is a biological signal whose cause still needs investigation; nucleosomes can also increase in some non-cancer illnesses.

The main validation study examined seven tumor groups: lymphoma, hemangiosarcoma, histiocytic sarcoma, melanoma, osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma and mast cell tumors.

Overall sensitivity was 49.8% at 97% specificity. Detection varied considerably: lymphoma, hemangiosarcoma and histiocytic sarcoma were detected more often, while sensitivity was about 19% for mast cell tumors and 35% for osteosarcoma.

“Detects seven cancers” therefore does not mean equal performance across all seven. Nor does the result identify which cancer is suspected or where it is. The study compared samples from dogs with known diagnoses and healthy dogs, rather than demonstrating the benefit of an annual screening program. Read the Nu.Q study.

IDEXX Cancer Dx: markers of lymphoma and mast cell tumors

Cancer Dx uses blood. The current US panel looks for signals associated with lymphoma and mast cell tumors.

IDEXX describes proprietary circulating biomarkers assessed using multiple analytical technologies. The open materials reviewed do not disclose the complete marker panel or assay design, so describing it simply as a tumor-DNA test would be inaccurate.

The company reports 79% sensitivity for lymphoma and 22–41% for mast cell tumors, with approximately 99% specificity for each component. For mast cell tumors, IDEXX emphasizes clinically aggressive cases.

The result gives the veterinarian a more specific direction than a general risk score. But the lower mast cell tumor sensitivity means many cases may be missed. A negative result does not justify ignoring a lump, and the panel does not rule out cancers it does not test for. Current panel and performance; medical evidence.

Oncotect: a biological response to urine

Oncotect uses microscopic worms called Caenorhabditis elegans as biological sensors. In the laboratory, their movement toward or away from a urine sample is measured. This response is called chemotaxis.

The idea is that cancer changes substances excreted in urine, and the worms respond to those chemical differences. The test does not identify a particular mutation or directly count cancer cells in urine.

The main cancer groups studied are lymphoma, hemangiosarcoma, mast cell tumors and melanoma. The published proof-of-concept study reported approximately 85% sensitivity and 90% specificity. The company separately reports 83% and 96% from a larger dataset; these are different bodies of evidence and should not be blended into one result.

The report estimates risk rather than identifying the cancer type or location. Urine collection is convenient, but a high-risk result still needs veterinary investigation. Published study; manufacturer's research summary.

CADET BRAF and BRAF-PLUS: genetic changes in urine

These tests primarily address urothelial carcinoma of the urinary tract and some prostate cancers.

CADET BRAF looks for a particular BRAF mutation in cellular material shed into urine. This is a molecular change associated with specific tumors, rather than a general marker of inflammation or cell damage.

Not every relevant tumor carries that mutation. Antech states that BRAF detects up to 85% of urothelial carcinoma cases. When BRAF is negative, BRAF-PLUS can look for other changes: abnormal numbers of copies of particular DNA regions.

The test may help investigate urinary signs or support screening in selected high-risk breeds. It is not a general cancer screen, and a negative result does not exclude every bladder tumor. Antech's clinical explanation; test description.

VDI Cancer Risk Assessment: cell division and inflammation

This blood test combines thymidine kinase 1, or TK1, an enzyme associated with DNA synthesis and cell proliferation, and C-reactive protein, or CRP, a marker of inflammation.

The combination is used to calculate a risk assessment. It does not identify a specific tumor or mutation; it measures processes that can accompany cancer but also occur in other conditions.

The manufacturer explicitly distinguishes the result from a cancer diagnosis and describes a risk horizon of the next four to six months. An elevated score therefore means the cause needs investigation; it does not establish which cancer is present, or that cancer is present at all. VDI's explanation of the markers and intended use.

Calviri StageOne Plus: the immune response to a tumor

StageOne Plus takes a different approach: examining antibody patterns associated with the immune response to cancer.

In the published study, serum was applied to microarrays containing many peptides, or short protein fragments. Researchers measured antibody binding and used the resulting patterns to classify samples.

The study examined five cancers at stage I: hemangiosarcoma, lymphoma, mast cell tumors, osteosarcoma and soft tissue sarcoma. There were 283 dogs, including 69 without cancer. Sensitivity ranged from 60% to 98%, depending on the model and cancer type. That range is not a claim of 98% accuracy for every cancer.

Studying stage I disease is an important strength. The limitation is that the models were evaluated using research samples; this is not yet a demonstration of the outcome of repeatedly screening apparently healthy dogs. The product page describes access through a limited number of clinics. Published study; Calviri's product information.

OncoK9: an important test that is no longer available

OncoK9 is no longer available. PetDx stopped processing samples on March 6, 2024 because of funding constraints and closed its commercial operations. It belongs here because its research remains important, not because owners can currently order it. Contemporaneous report quoting the company's notice; professional account of the closure.

OncoK9 used next-generation sequencing of cell-free DNA from blood to search for cancer-associated genetic changes. Unlike a measurement of total DNA, it examined alterations in the DNA itself.

In the interim CLASSiC analysis, 417 dogs had classifiable outcomes and 51 were diagnosed with cancer, including 14 presumptive diagnoses. Sensitivity was 56.9% and specificity 98.9%. Most participants were at increased risk because of age or breed.

Routine care identified six of the 51 cancers before symptoms appeared. Adding the blood test increased that number to 28. This demonstrates additional preclinical detection in that population, but not yet longer survival or less burdensome treatment. Publication of the results in 2026 does not mean the commercial service reopened. CLASSiC interim results.

LatusPet SINO: patterns in metabolism

SINO uses a blood sample. Nuclear magnetic resonance, or NMR, examines serum metabolites and lipoproteins. This is a laboratory analysis of the sample; the dog does not undergo an MRI scan.

A machine-learning model combines these measurements with blood-count information and age. It searches for patterns associated with disease rather than a specific tumor mutation.

The published cancer model used 34 dogs with cancer and 98 non-cancer controls, including dogs with other illnesses. The cancer group included lymphoma, carcinomas, mast cell tumors, melanoma and other neoplasms.

Internal cross-validation produced 86.5% sensitivity and 82.4% specificity. At that specificity, approximately 18 in 100 non-cancer dogs would be classified as positive under comparable conditions. Independent validation, particularly in apparently healthy dogs, is needed. The small study does not establish reliable performance for each individual cancer type. LatusPet study, 2026.

DeepScan CFD: the amount of cell-free DNA

DeepScan measures the amount of cell-free DNA in blood. DNA enters the circulation when cells are damaged or die. Increased concentrations can accompany cancer, inflammation and other diseases.

This differs from a genetic liquid biopsy: CFD measures the amount of material rather than identifying particular cancer mutations.

The company proposes using the marker to investigate hidden disease and follow trends over time. A rising level may prompt further tests; changes during treatment are interpreted alongside other clinical findings.

An elevated CFD result does not establish that cancer is the cause, identify the cancer type or locate a tumor. The manufacturer describes applications across cancer and non-cancer conditions. A single universal cancer-detection percentage would not adequately describe those different uses. DeepScan's explanation of CFD; limitations of a standalone result.

Canos Guardian: molecular signals in blood

Guardian analyzes cell-free DNA from a blood sample to look for signals from multiple cancer types. Canos describes a platform incorporating sequencing, DNA methylation and fragmentomics. Methylation refers to chemical marks on DNA; fragmentomics examines features of the DNA fragments.

The current Canos website lists Guardian as available to order. It also now includes cancer-signal detection within Origin, a broader genetic and epigenetic health profile. Earlier descriptions of Guardian as unavailable and Origin as a baseline-only product no longer match the live product pages.

However, the reviewed Guardian page does not provide its own detailed sensitivity table by cancer and stage. Links to other canine liquid-biopsy studies, including CANDiD, support the general approach; their performance figures cannot automatically be assigned to Guardian. Evaluating this particular product requires validation of this particular test. Guardian; Origin; information for veterinarians.

testblu CaniSense: the features of DNA fragments

CaniSense is being developed as a blood-based multi-cancer test. The company combines next-generation sequencing, fragmentomics and machine learning.

The idea is that the sequence is not the only useful information in circulating DNA. How DNA is fragmented and the patterns found across a sample may also help identify cancer-associated changes.

CaniSense Light is described as an initial screen for increased cancer risk. The active pilot collects blood and clinical information from dogs with suspected or confirmed cancer and healthy controls to develop and validate the technology.

It should therefore be described as a development and validation program. The reviewed pages do not provide completed clinical validation establishing reliable sensitivity for particular cancers or early stages. Technology overview; CaniSense pilot.

CanCan K9-LiquiDX: cancer-associated mutations in blood

K9-LiquiDX sequences cell-free DNA from blood to identify genetic changes associated with cancer. Unlike a total-DNA measurement, it examines the sequence itself.

The manufacturer describes uses in supporting diagnosis, monitoring treatment, detecting relapse and identifying changes that may help inform treatment decisions.

Detection depends on whether the tumor contains changes covered by the test and whether enough tumor DNA enters the blood. CanCan's technical guidance recommends adding tumor sequencing in some monitoring situations to improve sensitivity.

It should not be presented as an equally reliable screen for every cancer. The reviewed materials do not establish universal sensitivity and specificity for repeated screening of apparently healthy dogs. Its performance in a dog with a known tumor needs to be considered separately from preventive screening. K9-LiquiDX product information; technical FAQs.

Other approaches: risk research and assessment of a known lump

The University of Minnesota's LYRA and Shine On programs investigate lymphoma and hemangiosarcoma risk. Shine On also studies intervention before clinical disease. These remain research programs, not established routine cancer prevention.

HT Vet VISTA iQ uses heat-diffusion imaging and algorithms to assess skin and subcutaneous masses that have already been found. It addresses a different question from a blood or urine screen for hidden disease elsewhere in the body.

After the result: a clearer first step, an uncertain longer road

A positive screening result generally leads to further veterinary investigation. The veterinarian considers the particular signal, examines the dog and proposes appropriate next steps: sampling a lump or lymph node, ultrasound, radiographs or other investigations when indicated. Some manufacturers already provide guidance for this first stage, including IDEXX Cancer Dx interpretation algorithms and Nu.Q follow-up information.

The longer-term pathway is less clear, particularly when the signal persists but no tumor can be found. How long should the search continue? How often should examinations or imaging be repeated? When does the burden of further investigation outweigh the expected benefit?

Insurance is part of that uncertainty. Owners need to establish whether their particular policy covers the screening test, diagnostic work after a positive result and continued monitoring without a confirmed diagnosis. An offer of early detection does not itself guarantee payment for everything that follows.

Manufacturers, veterinarians and insurers need to develop that pathway together. Detecting a signal is an important first step. Making subsequent care understandable, affordable and supported by evidence remains unfinished work for much of this field.
