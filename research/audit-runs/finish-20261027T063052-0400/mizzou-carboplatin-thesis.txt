ABSTRACT
Canine appendicular osteosarcoma (OSA) is an aggressive primary bone tumor
characterized by early micrometastasis and high metastatic failure rates despite
multimodal therapy. Although adjuvant chemotherapy following limb amputation
improves survival, the optimal timing of chemotherapy initiation is still incompletely
defined, in part due to concern that early postoperative treatment may increase the risk of
surgical site infection (SSI) and disrupt wound healing. The objectives of this study were
to compare SSI rates between dogs receiving carboplatin within 5 days of amputation and
a matched historical cohort receiving carboplatin at least 11days postoperatively and to
evaluate progression-free interval (PFI) and overall survival time (OST) in dogs receiving
carboplatin within 5 days versus at least 11 days after surgery.
For the infection analysis, 12 prospectively enrolled dogs treated with carboplatin
within 1–4 days of amputation were compared with 21 matched historical controls. The
overall SSI rate was 24.2% (8/33), with similar rates in the historical and prospective
cohorts (23.8% [5/21] vs 25.0% [3/12]). When chemotherapy timing was dichotomized
(≤5 days vs >5 days), infection rates were nearly identical (25.0% vs 23.8%; P = 0.9999).
A three-category timing analysis (≤5, 5–15, >15 days) showed a statistically significant
association driven by a higher infection rate in dogs initiating chemotherapy >15 days
postoperatively (66.7%; P = 0.0135), consistent with chemotherapy delays occurring

viii

secondary to wound complications. Chemotherapy timing demonstrated weak
discrimination for predicting SSI (AUC = 0.6925). Tumor location, age category, sex,
amputation site (thoracic vs pelvic), and surgical duration were not significantly
associated with SSI.
For the survival analysis, 10 prospective dogs were compared with 15 matched
historical dogs. Median time to chemotherapy initiation was 1 day (1–4) in the
prospective cohort and 17 days (range 11–59 days) in historical controls. Median PFI was
125 days (range 63-237 days) in the prospective group and 184 days (range 62–650 days)
in the historical group; median OST was 236 days (range 122-321 days) and 270 days
(range 141–1037 days), respectively. No statistically significant differences in PFI or
OST were detected between early and later chemotherapy groups. Proximal humeral
tumor location was associated with shorter OST (median 171 vs 287 days; P = 0.0393).
In multivariable Cox regression models adjusted for age and chemotherapy timing,
neither treatment delay nor SSI was significantly associated with overall survival;
however, SSI showed a higher hazard ratio (HR 4.07; P = 0.12), showing a potentially
clinically relevant effect that justifies further study.
In conclusion, initiating carboplatin within 5 days of limb amputation did not
increase SSI risk and was not associated with statistically significant differences in PFI or
OST in this cohort. Larger, multicenter studies with standardized follow-up and treatment
guidelines are needed to better define the impact of postoperative complications and
chemotherapy timing on long-term outcomes in canine appendicular OSA.

INTRODUCTION & LITERATURE REVIEW
Osteosarcoma (OSA) is the most common primary bone malignancy in dogs,
comprising nearly 85% of skeletal tumors. This aggressive mesenchymal neoplasm arises
from primitive osteoblasts capable of producing osteoid. It is distinguished by rapid local
bone destruction, increased risk of pathologic fracture, and a high propensity for early
metastasis. Large and giant-breed dogs exhibit a disproportionately high incidence, with
recognized breed predispositions, including Scottish Deerhounds, Rottweilers, Great
Danes, Leonbergers, and Irish Setters, denoting a strong heritable component to disease
risk.1 The risk of OSA increases progressively with advancing age, greater body weight,
higher standardized weight, and greater standardized height. Standardized measurements
adjust height and weight relative to breed- and sex-specific expectations, allowing
comparison across dogs of different sizes. In a study evaluating risk estimates for both
standardized for height and weight, increasing height was a stronger and more consistent
predictor of OSA risk than weight, suggesting that skeletal stature is a particularly
significant predisposing factor.2 Several studies have reported a bimodal age distribution
of OSA in dogs, with an early incidence peak in animals around 18–24 months and a
larger, more common peak in middle-aged to older dogs (approximately 7–9 years).3 This
pattern, although not uniformly registered across all datasets, suggests that distinct
biological factors may contribute to tumor development across life stages in dogs, similar
in some respects to the bimodal distribution observed in human OSA, in which the
disease peaks in younger people (<20 years) and older adults (>60 years).4 Recent
genomic studies have identified recurrent alterations in RB1, TP53, PTEN, and

1

MAPK/PI3K signaling pathways, strengthening the biological parallels between canine
and human OSA and supporting the utility of the companion dog as a comparative
oncology model. 5
In contrast to the frequent occurrence of OSA in dogs, the disease occurs much
less frequently in humans. In the United States, approximately 800–1,000 new cases of
OSA are diagnosed in humans each year, representing a rare malignancy with an
incidence of roughly 0.9 cases per 100,000 people annually.4 In dogs, large
epidemiologic datasets indicate that OSA is considerably more common: a study of
394,061 insured dogs reported an incidence rate of 27.2 cases per 100,000 dogs per year,
which is substantially higher than in humans.4 The markedly elevated incidence of OSA
in dogs, coupled with shared tumor biology and clinical behavior, makes the companion
dog population a relevant naturally occurring comparative model for investigating human
OSA pathogenesis and therapy. Human OSA shows a consistent male predominance,
with incidence rates approximately 1.3–1.5 times higher in males than females across
multiple population-based studies. 6 The disease is also strongly associated with periods
of rapid skeletal growth, as reflected by its peak incidence during adolescence. Height
appears to be an important predisposing factor in young patients. Epidemiologic studies
have confirmed that individuals diagnosed with OSA tend to be taller than age-matched
peers, and a significant proportion are above the 50th percentile for height at diagnosis.7
A meta-analysis of six studies estimated that 62% (95% CI: 57–67%) of osteosarcoma
patients were taller than the median height for the reference population, supporting an
association between increased skeletal stature and osteosarcoma risk, with little variation
between studies (τ² = 0.006).8 Together, these results indicate that factors related to

2

longitudinal bone growth, skeletal development, and growth velocity may contribute to
OSA susceptibility in adolescents and young adults.
Standard staging for canine OSA continues to rely primarily on three-view
thoracic radiographs to evaluate for pulmonary metastases, the most common site of
disease spread. However, thoracic radiographs have limited sensitivity for detecting small
or early metastatic lesions. The increasing availability of thoracic and whole-body
computed tomography (CT), technetium-99m nuclear scintigraphy, and 18Ffluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG
PET/CT) offers the potential to improve detection of both pulmonary and osseous
metastases at the time of initial evaluation. Early veterinary studies show that advanced
imaging modalities, particularly PET/CT, may identify metastatic or additional neoplastic
lesions not evident on conventional radiographs or CT alone, implying increased
diagnostic sensitivity.9
In spite of advances in diagnostic imaging and surgical techniques, OSA is still a
systemic disease at diagnosis. In dogs, the lungs represent the predominant site of
metastatic spread, with a much smaller proportion, generally 10% or fewer, developing
metastases in other bones, regional lymph nodes, or visceral organs.5 Current literature
has suggested that approximately 80-90% of dogs harbor microscopic pulmonary
metastases at the time their primary tumor is diagnosed, with the remaining 10-20%
exhibiting detectable macroscopic metastatic disease.10 Because OSA is a highly
metastatic disease, long-term outcome is determined far more by control of distant
microscopic spread than by management of the primary tumor. Once adequate local
control is achieved, whether by limb amputation or limb-sparing surgery, metastatic

3

progression becomes the predominant life-limiting factor. Despite aggressive, multimodal
standard-of-care therapy, approximately 90% of dogs eventually develop recurrent or
metastatic disease following completion of chemotherapy, with the lungs representing the
most common site of relapse.11 This high rate of metastatic failure highlights why
systemic therapy, rather than local therapy alone, ultimately dictates survival.
In human OSA, similar to canine OSA, approximately 15–20% of patients present
with detectable metastatic disease at diagnosis, with lung involvement in about 80% of
metastatic cases.12 Even among those who achieve initial remission with multimodal
therapy, 30–50% ultimately develop recurrent or metastatic disease, with pulmonary
metastases representing the most common site of relapse.13 Once metastasis is
established, long-term outcomes remain poor; the mean 5-year overall survival for
patients with metastatic OSA is below 30%, demonstrating the key influence of
metastatic progression on prognosis and the need for improved systemic treatment
methods. 14,15
As a result, multimodal therapy is the standard of care, with limb amputation,
limb-sparing surgery, or radiation therapy addressing local disease, whereas systemic
chemotherapy is essential for prolonging survival. Limb amputation remains the standard
approach for local tumor control in dogs with appendicular OSA and is generally
associated with excellent functional results and quality of life, even in large and giantbreed dogs.16 Multiple studies have demonstrated that approximately 85–90% of dogs
preserve the same or near-same quality of life following amputation, and more than 70%
return to preoperative activity levels, with most patients rapidly adapting to three-limb
ambulation.16–18 Although progression of osteoarthritis in remaining limbs may occur, it

4

rarely results in clinically significant impairment, and postoperative rehabilitation may
further enhance functional recovery.16 Limb-salvage surgery (LSS) offers an alternative
for select patients, particularly those with nonmetastatic disease, tumors involving less
than 50% of the affected bone, most commonly the distal radius, and without pathologic
fracture or extensive soft tissue involvement.19 When combined with adjuvant
chemotherapy, LSS provides survival outcomes comparable to amputation18,20; however,
complication rates remain high, with postoperative infection reported in approximately
40–50% of cases, along with implant failure and local recurrence.16,18 Despite these risks,
limb function is reported as good to excellent in roughly 80% of dogs undergoing LSS.16
Notably, several studies have observed prolonged survival in dogs that develop
postoperative infections following LSS compared with non-infected dogs, a finding
mirrored in human OSA and believed to reflect immune-mediated antitumor effects.21
Radiation therapy is a well-established treatment option for dogs with
appendicular osteosarcoma, particularly when amputation or LSS is not pursued, and is
primarily used for pain relief and local tumor control. Early studies showed that coarsely
fractionated palliative radiation provides meaningful analgesia and improved limb
function in most dogs, although it does not prevent metastatic disease and overall survival
remains limited.22 More recent advances include hypofractionated protocols, which offer
effective palliation with acceptable toxicity, and stereotactic body radiation therapy
(SBRT), which has achieved improved local control and median survival times of
approximately 230 days, though metastatic progression remains the main cause of
treatment failure.23

5

Historical data show that for dogs, surgery alone yields median survival times of
approximately 4–5 months24, whereas the addition of adjuvant chemotherapy with agents
such as carboplatin, doxorubicin, or platinum-anthracycline combinations extends median
survival to 10–12 months and improves 1-year survival rates two- to three-fold.25,26
Several studies have compared commonly used adjuvant chemotherapy protocols for
canine appendicular OSA. In a randomized phase III trial, dogs treated with six doses of
carboplatin demonstrated a significantly longer disease-free interval (DFI) (425 vs. 135
days) compared with dogs receiving alternating carboplatin and doxorubicin, while
toxicity profiles were similar between treatment groups.27 Additionally, retrospective
comparisons of carboplatin, doxorubicin, and alternating carboplatin–doxorubicin
protocols have reported no statistically significant differences in overall DFI or survival
time (ST); however, a lower proportion of dogs treated with carboplatin alone
experienced adverse events, suggesting improved tolerability and quality of life with this
protocol. 26 Carboplatin is currently considered the standard first-line chemotherapeutic
agent for the treatment of OSA.
The management of OSA in humans also relies on a multimodal treatment
strategy incorporating systemic chemotherapy and surgical resection due to the
presumption of micrometastatic disease at the time of diagnosis.28 Neoadjuvant
chemotherapy is administered before surgical removal of the primary tumor and has
formed the foundation of OSA therapy since the late 20th century. The goals of this
approach include reducing the primary tumor size, improving the feasibility of limbsparing procedures, and initiating early systemic treatment for micrometastases.29,30 The
most widely used regimen is the MAP protocol, which includes high-dose methotrexate,

6

doxorubicin, and cisplatin.31 Response to neoadjuvant therapy, measured by the degree of
tumor necrosis in the resected specimen, is a strong predictor of outcome, with good
responders demonstrating significantly improved survival.32
Surgical excision of the primary tumor is still essential for cure. Developments in
imaging, chemotherapy, and reconstructive surgical techniques now allow limb-sparing
surgery in approximately 80–90% of patients.33 Amputation is still indicated when limbsparing margins cannot be safely achieved or when major neurovascular structures are
involved. Regardless of approach, achieving wide surgical margins is critical to reduce
local recurrence risk.32
Following surgery, patients resume systemic chemotherapy to eradicate residual
microscopic disease. Completion of full-dose, uninterrupted adjuvant chemotherapy is
strongly correlated with improved outcomes. Delays in postoperative chemotherapy,
reductions in dose intensity, or early treatment discontinuation markedly reduce eventfree and overall survival.34 Typical postoperative treatment extends the MAP regimen or
incorporates additional agents such as ifosfamide or etoposide in selected protocols.31
In veterinary oncology, adjuvant chemotherapy following limb amputation for
appendicular OSA is traditionally initiated approximately 10–14 days postoperatively.35
This delay aims to minimize the risk of wound dehiscence, infection, and delayed tissue
repair by postponing chemotherapy until the early phases of healing have progressed.36
Additionally, this timeframe permits postoperative review of the surgical site and
confirmation of adequate hematologic parameters before systemic treatment is
introduced. While widely adopted in clinical practice, this convention is primarily based

7

on theoretical concerns regarding wound healing rather than robust prospective data
evaluating optimal timing in canine OSA.36
Given the systemic nature of OSA and the dependence on timely multimodal
therapy to optimize outcomes, understanding factors that influence long-term survival is
critical. Several studies have explored the influence of postoperative chemotherapy
timing on survival outcomes in dogs with appendicular OSA, although the evidence
remains limited and somewhat inconsistent. Early experimental work showed a clear
survival benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy compared with surgery alone, but within
narrow early initiation windows, 2 versus 10 days post-amputation, no significant
difference in overall survival was observed, suggesting that modest early alterations in
timing may not significantly affect outcomes when chemotherapy is delivered.36 A larger
retrospective multicenter analysis reported that dogs initiating adjuvant chemotherapy
within five days of amputation had numerically longer median OST and PFI than dogs
that began treatment later; however, in multivariable analysis, timing was not an
independent predictor of outcome after adjusting for other variables.35 These studies
show a lack of conclusive evidence for an optimal postoperative interval for
chemotherapy in canine OSA and suggest that, while early systemic therapy is critical,
modest variations in timing do not independently affect survival. The limited number of
prospective evaluations, variation in chemotherapy protocols, and potential confounding
by postoperative complications demonstrate the need for well-designed prospective
studies to clarify the impact of chemotherapy timing on long-term outcomes in this
disease.

Consequently, the objectives of this study were to evaluate the association
between the development of surgical site infection after early chemotherapy administered
within 5 days of amputation and OSA in dogs, and to explore how the timing of initial
chemotherapy administration relates to subsequent survival outcomes.

BACKGROUND
Multiple retrospective studies have characterized the incidence and risk factors for
postoperative wound complications following limb amputation in dogs, providing
important context for evaluating SSI risk in OSA patients. It has been reported that shortterm wound complications occur in approximately 30–35% of dogs undergoing limb
amputation, with the majority of complications developing within the first two weeks
postoperatively.37 Identified risk factors included pelvic limb amputation, longer surgical
duration, and increased body weight, while age, sex, and underlying diagnosis, including
OSA, were not significantly associated with complication risk.37 Similarly, another study
documented an SSI rate of approximately 20–25% following canine limb amputation and
identified increased body weight and longer surgical time as significant predictors of
infection. Importantly, no significant association was found between SSI and age, sex, or
primary diagnosis, supporting the conclusion that mechanical and procedural factors,
rather than patient demographics or disease etiology, are primary drivers of postoperative
infection risk.38 Together, these studies establish limb amputation as a procedure with a
relatively high risk of early wound complications and SSI, particularly in large-breed
dogs, and emphasize the importance of postoperative wound healing considerations when
planning adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy.

10

While the benefit of chemotherapy is well established, the optimal timing of its
initiation is still incompletely defined. The established recommendation of initiating
chemotherapy is within 10–14 days following amputation, but delays in chemotherapy
start are common and may be driven by postoperative complications, or owner decisionmaking. Retrospective studies suggest that prolonged intervals between surgery and
chemotherapy may permit expansion of micrometastatic clones; however, published
findings are inconsistent. 29,39
Concerns regarding postoperative infection influence clinical decision-making
and contribute to variability in treatment timing. Wound healing is a coordinated process
involving inflammation, cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix
remodeling, all of which may be disrupted by cytotoxic chemotherapy.40 Chemotherapymediated complications of wound healing have been increasingly recognized as an
important but understudied adverse effect of cancer treatment. Cytotoxic agents impair
fibroblast migration and proliferation, suppress immune cell function, reduce collagen
synthesis, and inhibit neovascularization, collectively delaying tissue repair and
weakening wound integrity.41 Platinum-based agents, including cisplatin and carboplatin,
exert direct cytotoxic effects on rapidly dividing cells within healing tissue and induce
oxidative stress, additionally compromising re-epithelialization and tensile strength.42
These effects are most pronounced when chemotherapy is administered during the early
postoperative period, when inflammatory and proliferative phases of healing are most
active. As a result, patients receiving systemic chemotherapy are at increased risk for
delayed healing, wound dehiscence, and surgical site infection, providing a biologic basis
for historical concerns regarding early postoperative chemotherapy initiation.41 Although

11

prospective veterinary data specifically evaluating perioperative chemotherapy and
wound healing are limited, these biologic effects support the rationale for delaying
systemic chemotherapy in the immediate postoperative period to minimize potential
impairment of wound healing.
The purpose of this arm of the study was to compare the rate of SSI between a
group of historical patients with appendicular OSA who received carboplatin
chemotherapy at least 11 days after amputation versus a prospective group of patients
who received carboplatin chemotherapy within 5 days of amputation. A secondary aim of
this arm of the study was to evaluate if age, sex, tumor location (thoracic vs pelvic limb),
or length of surgical procedure correlated with increased risk of SSI. We tested the
hypothesis that early administration would increase the risk of SSI post-amputation.

METHODS
Historical patient selection
The medical records for dogs diagnosed with appendicular OSA treated with
amputation followed by 4-6 doses of carboplatin chemotherapy between January 2016
and December 2024 at a single institution (University of Missouri Veterinary Health
Center) were reviewed. Patients who received amputation at the University of Missouri
Veterinary Health Center by a board-certified surgeon, followed by 4-6 doses of
carboplatin initiated at least 11 days post-surgery, were evaluated for the occurrence of
postoperative infection.
Initial staging was variable but included a physical exam, complete blood count,
serum chemistry profile, urinalysis, limb radiographs, thoracic radiographs, and cytology

12

or tumor biopsy. Some patients underwent additional imaging, including thoracic CT,
abdominal imaging (radiographs, ultrasound, or CT), PET/CT, and nuclear
scintigraphy. Histopathology was reviewed for all patients, and histologic subtypes of
OSA were recorded when applicable. Dogs were excluded if their final histopathologic
diagnosis was not consistent with OSA, if they had amputation performed at a different
institution, if they had evidence of metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis, or if they
had axial or extraskeletal OSA. Dogs with incomplete medical records, including blood
work before amputation, as well as histopathology of the primary tumor, were excluded.
All patients had pertinent information recorded, including signalment, date of diagnosis,
location of the primary tumor, blood work, imaging findings, cytology results,
histopathology results, date of amputation, date of carboplatin treatment initiation, length
of the amputation procedure, and any postoperative surgical complications.
Prospective patient selection
The prospective group of dogs consisted of patients with suspected appendicular
OSA who presented for treatment at the University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center
between August 2024 and August 2025. Animal use was approved by the University of
Missouri’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Dogs were required to have a
cytological diagnosis of sarcoma with positive alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining or
OSA, or a histopathologic diagnosis of OSA before enrollment. Initial staging included,
at a minimum, thoracic and limb radiographs, tumor cytology, complete blood count, and
serum biochemistry. Dogs underwent amputation with the intention to follow with six
doses of carboplatin at a target dose of 300 mg/m2 administered once every 3 weeks,
starting within 5 days of amputation. A Complete Blood Count (CBC) was performed 7

13

days and 14 days following the first dose of carboplatin, then at the identified nadir for
subsequent doses. Dogs had their 14-day CBC performed at the University of Missouri
Veterinary Health Center, along with a recheck exam with the surgical oncologist to
assess for any postoperative complications.
Surgical Site Assessment for the Historical Group
The surgical site was assessed by a board-certified surgeon between 10 and 18
days postoperatively. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed for any indication
of surgical site complications, including draining tracts, purulent discharge, dehiscence,
seroma, scabbing, redness, swelling, crusting, and/or delayed chemotherapy. If an
aerobic/anaerobic culture and sensitivity was performed, the bacteria present and the
treatment instituted were recorded.
Surgical Site Assessment for the Prospective Group
The surgical site was assessed by a board-certified surgeon on days 1 and 14
postoperatively, and sometimes on day 7 if there were surgical site concerns, using an
incision evaluation form. The patient’s weight, temperature, heart rate, and respiratory
rate were recorded, and photos of the incision were taken. The following surgical site
parameters were monitored and recorded: discharge, redness, dehiscence, odor, swelling,
bruising, seroma, and any other findings. It was also recorded whether the patient
received bupivacaine liposome injectable suspension (Nocita®; Elanco US.)
intraoperatively and the patient’s pain score at the time. If there were any surgical site
complications, it was recorded whether an Elizabethan collar was always present,
whether the patient was licking or scratching the incision, whether the patient was
exercise-restricted at home, and whether the owner had concerns about the patient’s

14

incision site. If patients had complications, an incision evaluation form was completed
each time they were presented for evaluation. If there was concern for infection, a swab
for culture and sensitivity was obtained. If infection was identified, appropriate
antimicrobials were used based on culture and sensitivity results, and surgical site
dehiscence was managed with open wound care or primary closure.
Data Analysis
The relationship between the timing of chemotherapy initiation and postoperative
SSI was evaluated using nonparametric tests, logistic regression, and receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Because the interval between amputation and first
chemotherapy treatment, and other continuous variables were not normally distributed,
comparisons between dogs that developed an SSI and those that did not were performed
using the Mann-Whitney U-test. To identify factors associated with postoperative
infection, logistic regression was performed with SSI (yes/no) as the binary dependent
variable. Univariable logistic regression was used to screen for individual predictors,
including timing of chemotherapy after amputation, age, sex, and tumor location. Factors
with p-values less than or equal to 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
To evaluate whether SSI was associated with amputation site (thoracic versus
pelvic limb), a Fisher’s exact test was performed. The association between surgery
duration and SSI was assessed using a Wilcoxon two-sample test.
To assess whether the time interval between amputation and initiation of
chemotherapy (TIamp-chemo) could reliably distinguish between dogs that did and did not
develop a surgical site infection, ROC curve analysis was performed with TIamp-chemo
treated as a continuous variable. ROC analysis evaluates a predictor's ability to correctly

15

classify outcomes across a range of threshold values by plotting sensitivity (true-positive
rate) against specificity (false-positive rate). By analyzing TIamp-chemo in this manner, the
discriminatory performance of chemotherapy timing as a predictor of infection risk could
be quantified independently of any single cut-point. The resulting area under the curve
(AUC) provides a summary measure of predictive accuracy, with values closer to 1.0
indicating stronger discrimination and values near 0.5 indicating no better performance
than chance.

DISCUSSION
In this study, early initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy was not associated with an
increased risk of SSI. When chemotherapy timing was dichotomized as initiation within 5
days versus after 5 days, infection rates were nearly identical between groups, supporting
the clinical conclusion that administration of carboplatin shortly after limb amputation
does not appear to increase postoperative infection risk. This finding is particularly
relevant given the common concern that early chemotherapy may impair wound healing
and predispose patients to infection.
When chemotherapy timing was categorized as ≤5 days, 5–15 days, or>15 days, a
statistically significant association with infection was observed, driven by a markedly
higher infection rate in dogs receiving chemotherapy more than 15 days postoperatively.
Importantly, this finding is most likely explained by the fact that dogs that developed SSI
or wound complications often experienced delays in chemotherapy initiation as a result of
these complications. In this context, delayed chemotherapy is more likely a consequence
of infection rather than a causal factor.
Postoperative management factors also appeared to contribute to the risk of
infection. In all three of the dogs in the prospective group who developed an SSI, they
22

were able to lick or traumatize the incision site despite the use of an Elizabethan collar,
demonstrating the role of self-trauma as a potentially modifiable contributor to wound
complications such as dehiscence, seroma formation, and infection. Information
regarding postoperative management, particularly compliance with Elizabethan collar
use, was incomplete or inconsistently documented in the historical cohort, limiting our
ability to fully evaluate its possible contribution to surgical site complications. These
findings stress that strict adherence to postoperative restrictions and effective prevention
of incision interference are important components of surgical aftercare and may have a
greater impact on SSI risk than chemotherapy timing alone.
The overall SSI rate observed in this study (~25%) is consistent with previously
reported complication rates following canine limb amputation. Prior studies have
demonstrated that amputation carries a substantial risk of wound complications, with
reported SSI rates ranging from approximately 20–30%, regardless of underlying
diagnosis.37,38 It has been identified that postoperative complications following
amputation are often driven by mechanical and surgical factors, including incision
location, soft tissue handling, dead space, and postoperative motion, rather than by
oncologic treatment alone.37,38 This study did not find that the surgical procedure length
correlated with the risk of SSI.
Collectively, these results support the growing body of evidence that early
postoperative chemotherapy can be safely administered in appropriately selected patients,
provided that meticulous surgical technique, vigilant incision monitoring, and strict
postoperative management are employed.

EVALUATION OF SURVIVAL DATA FOR A HISTORICAL GROUP OF PATIENTS
WHO RECEIVED CARBOPLATIN AT LEAST 11 DAYS POSTOPERATIVELY
VERSUS A PROSPECTIVE GROUP OF PATIENTS WHO RECEIVED
CARBOPLATIN WITHIN 5 DAYS OF SURGERY

BACKGROUND
OSA is a highly aggressive primary bone tumor in both humans and dogs,
characterized by early microscopic metastasis and a dependence on timely systemic
therapy for long-term disease control. In human oncology, the importance of
chemotherapy timing is well established and has shaped contemporary multimodal
protocols. Foundational trials in the 1970s and early 1980s demonstrated that delaying
systemic therapy until after surgical resection permitted progression of micrometastatic
disease, causing a shift toward neoadjuvant chemotherapy.29 Prior to this period,
treatment consisted primarily of surgical resection alone, with survival rates rarely
exceeding 15–20% due to rapid development of pulmonary metastases. Early multiinstitutional trials demonstrated that adding multi-agent chemotherapy to surgical
resection dramatically improved outcomes in human OSA, with 6-year overall survival
increasing from approximately 11% with surgery alone to 61% with surgery combined
with systemic chemotherapy.31 These investigations revealed that postponing systemic
therapy until after tumor removal allowed microscopic metastatic deposits to proliferate
unchecked, whereas early chemotherapy effectively suppressed subclinical metastatic
disease. In addition to improving overall survival, preoperative chemotherapy produced

24

substantial tumor necrosis within the primary lesion, providing both cytoreduction to
facilitate limb-sparing surgery and a powerful prognostic indicator of treatment
response.29 Subsequent cooperative group analyses further established the importance of
limiting delays in both pre- and postoperative chemotherapy.32,34 In a comprehensive
analysis of 1,702 patients with high-grade OSA treated on neoadjuvant Cooperative
Osteosarcoma Study Group (COSS) protocols, it was demonstrated that histologic
response to preoperative chemotherapy and achievement of complete surgical remission
were among the strongest independent predictors of both event-free survival and OST.
The degree of tumor necrosis following neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been shown to be
a strong prognostic factor, with patients achieving ≥90% necrosis experiencing
substantially improved long-term outcomes, with a 5-year overall survival of 80% for
good responders versus 55% for poor responders, stressing the critical role of effective
preoperative systemic therapy. Importantly, all patients were treated using structured
protocols that incorporated both pre- and post-operative chemotherapy, reinforcing a
treatment paradigm in which OSA is approached as a systemic disease from diagnosis
and managed through continuous multimodal therapy. Although chemotherapy timing
was not analyzed as an independent variable, the strong association among treatment
response, completion of planned therapy, and survival outcomes supports the principle
that interruptions or delays in chemotherapy delivery, before or after surgery, may
compromise disease control. 32
A large retrospective analysis of 703 patients with localized extremity OSA found
that longer intervals between definitive surgery and the resumption of chemotherapy
were associated with poorer overall survival, with 5-year overall survival rates of 50-55%

25

versus 65-70% in patients without delays. Specifically, patients whose chemotherapy was
resumed more than 21 days after surgery had a significantly increased risk of death
compared with those who restarted within 21 days (hazard ratio = 1.57; p = 0.03).
Additionally, even delays beyond 16 days were linked to a trend toward worse outcomes,
particularly among patients with good histologic response to preoperative
chemotherapy.34
In veterinary medicine, adjuvant chemotherapy following limb amputation is
considered the standard of care for dogs with appendicular OSA; however, the optimal
timing of chemotherapy initiation remains poorly defined. Historically, most canine
patients begin chemotherapy approximately 10–14 days after amputation, a schedule
driven by surgical wound healing needs and longstanding clinical convention rather than
evidence-based evaluation of oncologic outcomes. Early clinical experience consisted of
a randomized clinical trial evaluating the effect of postoperative chemotherapy timing on
survival in dogs with appendicular OSA. This study enrolled 102 dogs that underwent
limb amputation and were assigned to receive adjuvant cisplatin- and doxorubicin-based
chemotherapy beginning either 2 days postoperatively (n=53) or 10 days postoperatively
(n=49), with outcomes compared to a historical control group treated with amputation
alone (n=162). Dogs receiving chemotherapy experienced a marked survival benefit
compared with surgery alone, with MSTs of approximately 11–12 months versus 5–6
months, respectively. However, dogs receiving adjuvant chemotherapy beginning 2 days
versus 10 days following limb amputation demonstrated similar median OSTs
(approximately 11.5 months vs 11 months, respectively) showing no statistically
significant difference in OST. Reported adverse effects were consistent with expected

26

chemotherapy-related toxicities, including transient gastrointestinal signs and
myelosuppression, with no clear increase in wound complications. Six of 53 dogs
receiving chemotherapy 2 days after amputation and 2 of 49 dogs receiving
chemotherapy 10 days after amputation died or were euthanized due to
myelosuppression; however, this was not significant. However, several limitations affect
the interpretation of these findings, including the relatively small sample size, use of
chemotherapy protocols that differ from current standards of care, reliance on a historical
control cohort, and limited power to detect subtle differences in postoperative
complications or long-term survival. Additionally, a detailed standardized assessment of
surgical site healing was not performed, which limits conclusions about the infection risk
associated with early chemotherapy initiation.36
In contrast, a multicenter retrospective study addressed several of these
limitations by evaluating a population of dogs with nonmetastatic appendicular OSA
treated using modern chemotherapy regimens, predominantly carboplatin-based protocols
consistent with current standards of care. Only dogs receiving systemic chemotherapy
were included and directly compared early versus delayed postoperative initiation within
the same treated population, thereby removing dependence on historical controls. In this
study, 168 dogs with nonmetastatic appendicular OSA that underwent limb amputation
followed by adjuvant chemotherapy were stratified by timing of chemotherapy initiation:
52 dogs received chemotherapy within 5 days of surgery, and 116 began treatment more
than 5 days postoperatively. Dogs that began chemotherapy within 5 days of surgery had
a median OST of 445 days (95% CI, 345–545 days), compared with 239 days (95% CI,
186–291 days) for dogs that initiated chemotherapy more than 5 days postoperatively.

27

Similarly, median time to tumor progression was longer in the early treatment group (375
days vs 202 days). Despite these numerical differences, multivariable analysis showed
that chemotherapy timing was not an independent predictor of survival.35 Early initiation
of chemotherapy was not associated with an increased rate of postoperative
complications or chemotherapy-related adverse events. Reported toxicities were
consistent with expected carboplatin effects, including transient gastrointestinal signs and
mild to moderate myelosuppression, with no significant difference in treatment
tolerability between early and delayed groups. Importantly, postoperative wound
complications were not significantly increased in dogs receiving early chemotherapy,
directly addressing longstanding clinical concerns regarding impaired healing. The
majority of limitations came from the retrospective nature of the study, which included
variability in surgical technique, postoperative monitoring, staging procedures, and
supportive care across participating institutions. Chemotherapy protocols, while largely
carboplatin-based, were not entirely uniform in dose or schedule. Additionally, reasons
for delayed chemotherapy initiation were not standardized and may have included
subclinical wound issues, logistical factors, or owner decision-making, introducing
potential confounding. In contrast to human OSA, veterinary literature provides limited
consensus regarding optimal treatment strategies.
As therapies advance and the emphasis on precision and evidence-based
veterinary oncology grows, clarifying the optimal interval between surgery and the
initiation of chemotherapy is increasingly essential. A prospective evaluation of
postoperative chemotherapy timing in dogs offers the opportunity to control confounding
variables, reduce biases inherent to retrospective designs, and generate robust clinical

28

evidence. Such data would directly inform best practices, improve clinical outcomes, and
strengthen comparative oncology models.
This thesis intends to address these questions by investigating the relationship
between the postoperative interval to chemotherapy and clinical outcomes in a
prospective group of dogs with appendicular OSA receiving chemotherapy within 5 days
postoperatively, compared to a historical group of dogs receiving standard chemotherapy
initiated at least 11 days postoperatively at the University of Missouri Veterinary Health
Center. This work aims to clarify whether timing of chemotherapy initiation is an
essential factor in therapeutic success and to provide evidence-based recommendations
for optimizing adjuvant treatment in canine OSA. We hypothesized that patients
receiving chemotherapy within 5 days would have longer DFI and OST than dogs
receiving chemotherapy at least 11 days postoperatively.
METHODS
Historical patient selection
The medical records for dogs diagnosed with appendicular OSA treated with
amputation followed by 6 doses of carboplatin chemotherapy between January 2016 and
December 2024 at a single institution (University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center)
were reviewed.
Initial staging was variable but included a physical exam, complete blood count,
serum chemistry profile, urinalysis, limb radiographs, thoracic radiographs, and cytology
or tumor biopsy. Some patients underwent additional imaging, including thoracic CT,
abdominal imaging (radiographs, ultrasound, or CT), PET/CT, and nuclear
scintigraphy. Histopathology was reviewed for all patients, and histologic subtypes of
29

OSA were recorded when applicable. Dogs were excluded if their final histopathology
did not support a diagnosis of OSA. Patients were excluded if they had amputation
performed at a different institution, if they had evidence of metastatic disease at the time
of diagnosis, axial or extraskeletal OSA, or if they had been treated with prior therapy,
including radiation therapy or immunogenic vaccines, concurrently or following
completion of carboplatin chemotherapy. Dogs with incomplete medical records,
including blood work before amputation, as well as histopathology of the primary tumor,
were excluded. This historical group of patients was included in the analysis if they
received carboplatin chemotherapy, initiated at least 11 days after surgery. All patients
had pertinent information recorded, including signalment, date of diagnosis, location of
primary tumor, blood work, imaging findings, cytology results, histopathology results,
date of amputation, date of carboplatin treatment initiation, and were followed until death
or lost to follow-up. If a dog developed metastatic disease, the location of the OSA
metastasis was recorded. Cause of death was recorded when available. When not
specified, dogs with known metastatic disease at the time of last contact prior to
euthanasia were presumed to have died from progressive disease. Dogs that were alive or
had no known metastatic disease at the point of last contact and dogs who died of causes
other than OSA were censored from survival analysis.
Prospective patient selection
The prospective group of dogs consisted of patients with suspected appendicular
OSA who presented for treatment at the University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center
between August 2024 and August 2025. Animal use was approved by the University of
Missouri’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Dogs were required

30

to have a cytological diagnosis of sarcoma with positive ALP staining or OSA, or
histologic diagnosis prior to enrollment. Initial staging included, at a minimum, thoracic
radiographs, limb radiographs, cytology of tumor, CBC, and serum biochemistry. Dogs
underwent amputation with the intention to follow with 6 doses of carboplatin at a target
dose of 300 mg/m2 administered once every 3 weeks, starting within 5 days of
amputation. CBCs were performed 7 days and 14 days after the first dose of carboplatin,
then at the identified nadir for subsequent doses. Dogs had their 14-day CBC performed
at the University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center, along with a recheck exam with
the surgical oncologist to assess for any postoperative complications. Thoracic
radiographs were performed prior to amputation and repeated prior to the third and sixth
doses of carboplatin, then every 3 months following during active surveillance. It was
recorded when dogs developed metastatic disease confirmed on radiographs or other
imaging as available. Dogs that developed metastatic disease were withdrawn from the
study and were eligible to receive additional treatment.
All patients had pertinent information recorded, including signalment, date of
diagnosis, location of primary tumor, blood work, imaging findings, cytology results,
histopathology results, date of amputation, date of carboplatin treatment initiation, and
were followed until death. If a dog developed metastatic disease, the location of the OSA
metastasis was recorded. Cause of death was recorded when available. When not
specified, dogs with known metastatic disease at the time of last contact prior to
euthanasia were presumed to have died from progressive disease. Dogs that were alive or
had no known metastatic disease at the point of last contact and dogs who died of causes
other than OSA were censored from survival analysis.

31

Data Analysis
Disease progression and survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier
method. PFI was calculated as the number of days from the cytologic or histopathologic
diagnosis to the identification of metastatic disease based on clinical impression, imaging
findings, cytological, or histopathological confirmation. OST was calculated as the
number of days from the time of cytologic or histopathologic diagnosis to death. Logrank test was performed on Kaplan-Meier survival curves to evaluate the influence of
chemotherapy initiation timing, second treatment delays, presence of infection at the
surgical site, tumor location, and initial alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level (if it was
elevated above the reference range), initial monocyte level >400k/uL, presence of
pathologic fracture prior to amputation, age and sex on PFI and OST. Factors with a pvalue less than or equal to 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
Cox proportional hazards regression modeling was performed to evaluate the
association between treatment delay and infection with PFS and OST, while controlling
for age and time to chemotherapy initiation. Cox-PH models estimate the relative hazard
of an event occurring over time and allow adjustment for possible confounding variables.
The resulting maximum likelihood estimates were used to calculate hazard ratios and
95% confidence intervals, providing a measure of the independent effect of treatment
delay on survival outcomes after accounting for age and chemotherapy timing.

or OST than dogs receiving chemotherapy at least 11 days postoperatively (Fig. 3 .5).

Proximal humeral location was associated with a statistically shorter median OST
(171 days, p = 0.0393) compared to tumors in other locations (OST 287 days; figure

Intact males were associated with a significantly shorter median PFI (106 days,

There was no statistical difference in OST or PFI for patients with early
chemotherapy timing, treatment delays, infection, elevated ALP, monocyte count
>400/uL, pathological fracture, or age (figures 3.5-3.11).
Table 3 reveals multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression that was used to
assess the association between second treatment delay, time to chemotherapy initiation,
and age with OST. After controlling chemotherapy timing and age, second treatment
delay was not significantly associated with survival (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.58, p = 0.42).
Similarly, time to chemotherapy initiation was not associated with overall survival (HR =
1.01, p = 0.48). Increasing age was associated with a trend toward increased hazard of
death, with an estimated 25% increase in risk per additional year of age (HR = 1.25),
although this effect did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.082).
Table 4 reveals Cox proportional hazards regression that was performed to
evaluate the association between surgical site infection and OST, while controlling for
age and time to chemotherapy initiation. Dogs that developed a surgical site infection had
a higher estimated risk of death compared with dogs without infection (hazard ratio [HR]
= 4.07); however, this association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12). Neither

time to chemotherapy initiation (HR = 0.99, p = 0.51) nor age (HR = 1.21, p = 0.14) was
significantly associated with overall survival in this model.
DISCUSSION
Although no statistically significant differences in OST or PFI were observed
between groups, interpretation of these findings is limited by the shorter follow-up
duration in the prospective cohort compared to the historical group (median follow-up
period of 236 days vs 337 days, respectively). As a result, late events and long-term
survivors may not yet have been captured, potentially leading to immature survival
estimates. Continued follow-up of this cohort is therefore necessary to better assess longterm outcomes and determine whether survival differs with longer observation.
Survival analyses suggested trends that were not statistically significant, rather
than a definitive absence of association. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards
modeling showed no statistically significant effect of treatment delay or SSI on overall
survival, after adjusting for age and chemotherapy timing. However, the hazard ratio for
infection in the overall survival model was approximately 4.07, showing a potentially
clinically meaningful increase in risk that did not reach statistical significance, likely due
to limited sample size and power.
Age also appeared to influence survival outcomes. Although not statistically
significant, age demonstrated a trend toward increased hazard in at least one
multivariable model (HR ~1.25; p ≈ 0.082), consistent with prior literature suggesting
that older dogs may experience worse outcomes independent of treatment variables. 43

37

This trend emphasizes age as a potential confounder and prognostic factor that merits
further investigation in larger cohorts.
Tumor location similarly influenced survival. Dogs with proximal humeral OSA
exhibited shorter OST than at other anatomic sites, a finding that is clinically plausible
given the increased surgical complexity, greater soft-tissue disruption, and higher
mechanical stress associated with proximal humeral amputations. The association
between proximal humeral location and poorer survival may also partly reflect
differences in tumor burden at diagnosis. Tumors arising in the proximal humerus are
often larger in volume because the surrounding musculature and soft tissue can
accommodate substantial tumor growth before lameness becomes severe. As a result,
these tumors may be biologically more advanced at the time of diagnosis, which could
contribute to their historically poorer prognosis. This observation is consistent with
previous reports identifying tumor location as an important determinant of outcome,
though the small number of cases limits definitive conclusions.
Male intact dogs demonstrated shorter progression-free intervals in this study;
however, this observation was based on a single male intact dog and therefore cannot be
generalized. Likewise, elevated ALP activity and monocyte counts >400/µL, which have
been associated with prognosis in prior studies 43, were not significantly associated with
outcome in this cohort, likely reflecting insufficient power rather than true lack of effect.
These data suggest that although no statistically significant survival differences
were identified, several variables, including infection status, age, and tumor location,
showed trends that may be clinically meaningful and need further investigation. Larger,

38

prospectively designed studies with longer follow-up and standardized treatment
guidelines will be necessary to more definitively characterize the impact of perioperative
factors on survival in canine appendicular OSA.
Although this study provides clinically relevant insight into chemotherapy timing,
SSI, and survival outcomes in dogs with appendicular OSA, several limitations must be
considered when interpreting the results. First, the small sample size limited statistical
power, particularly for multivariable analyses and subgroup comparisons, reducing the
ability to detect meaningful associations. As a result, associations that may be clinically
meaningful, such as the observed increase in the hazard of OS with infection, may not
have reached statistical significance. The inclusion of both historical and prospective
cohorts also introduced heterogeneity in case management, postoperative monitoring, and
clinical documentation. Differences in perioperative care, follow-up schedules, and
record completeness between cohorts may have influenced both the detection of
complications and treatment decisions, thereby confounding comparisons. In particular,
the definition of SSI relied primarily on clinical assessment, and culture confirmation was
not consistently available for dogs in the historical cohort. This may have resulted in
misclassification, underreporting, or overreporting of infection and limited the ability to
distinguish confirmed from suspected cases.
Additionally, post-metastatic treatments were not standardized across patients,
reflecting clinical practice, which complicates the interpretation of the outcomes of OST.
Variability in salvage therapies, including additional chemotherapy or palliative
interventions, may have influenced survival independently of perioperative factors such
as chemotherapy timing or SSI, thereby obscuring potential associations. A 2022 study
39

showed that treating dogs with macroscopic metastatic OSA with toceranib phosphate in
combination with losartan resulted in a clinical benefit rate of approximately 50% and a
median PFI of 111 days, supporting a potential role for tumor microenvironment
modulation in the management of advanced disease.44 Prior to this publication, several
chemotherapy drugs, such as ifosfamide, doxorubicin, cisplatin, mitoxantrone and
paclitaxel, were reported for rescue treatment for metastatic OSA; however, the prognosis
was only 1-2 months with treatment.45,46 It is possible that many owners elected not to
pursue rescue treatment due to poor outcomes with metastatic OSA.47 Because the
combination of toceranib phosphate and losartan has only recently been described as a
treatment option for dogs with metastatic OSA, it was not routinely used during much of
the period covered by the historical cohort. As a result, dogs in the historical group were
less likely to receive this regimen following metastatic progression, whereas several dogs
in the prospective cohort received this combination as rescue therapy. This evolution in
therapeutic practice introduces the potential for selection and treatment bias, as access to
newer therapies may have influenced post-metastatic disease control and overall survival
independent of perioperative chemotherapy timing or surgical site infection status.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
This study evaluated the relationship between the timing of adjuvant
chemotherapy initiation following limb amputation, SSI, and survival outcomes in dogs
with appendicular OSA. Overall, early initiation of chemotherapy was not associated
with an increased risk of SSI, and infection rates were comparable between dogs
receiving chemotherapy within five days of surgery and those treated later. This data
suggests that routine delays in chemotherapy initiation solely out of concern for
postoperative infection may be unnecessary in clinically stable patients with appropriate
incision healing.
Although no statistically significant differences in OST or PFI were identified,
interpretation of survival outcomes was limited by small sample size and shorter followup in the prospective cohort, which may have precluded detection of late events and longterm survivors. Multivariable survival analyses demonstrated no independent association
between treatment delay or SSI and survival; however, observed effect sizes, particularly
the increased hazard associated with infection, suggest the possibility of clinically
meaningful trends that need further investigation. Additional factors, including age and
tumor location, appeared to influence outcomes.
Collectively, these findings add to the growing body of veterinary oncology
literature indicating that the delivery of adjuvant chemotherapy itself, rather than modest
variation in postoperative timing, is the most critical determinant of outcome.

41

Importantly, this work helps address a persistent area of uncertainty in veterinary OSA
management, where research-based recommendations on optimal perioperative
chemotherapy timing remain limited.
Future research needs to prioritize prospective, well-powered multicenter studies
to better determine how chemotherapy timing and postoperative complications affect
cancer outcomes in dogs with appendicular OSA. Consistent and extended follow-up
periods will be important for accurately evaluating long-term survival and detecting late
events that may influence OST and PFI. Further research should also explore the
prognostic significance of patient-specific and tumor-related factors, including age, tumor
location, and biochemical markers, in conjunction with treatment variables. The use of
more sensitive staging techniques and consistent evaluation of metastatic disease at
diagnosis may improve characterization of disease burden. Ultimately, larger, longerterm prospective studies will be necessary to develop evidence-based clinical guidelines
for perioperative chemotherapy administration and to establish outcomes for dogs with
appendicular OSA.
