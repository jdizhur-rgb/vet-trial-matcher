# Evidence disclosure correction — October 10, 2026

Owner found the Evidence text uninformative and requested correction. All 13 disclosures now state the actual result and its limitation. Sources checked during correction:

- APAVAC / Vaxkit: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24300788/
  - Result: In a randomized study of 19 dogs with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, median lymphoma-specific survival was 505 days with vaccine plus chemotherapy and 159 days with chemotherapy alone.
  - Limit: The result is encouraging, but the trial was small and studied one lymphoma subtype. It does not establish the same benefit for every dog or chemotherapy protocol.
- Aurelius adoptive T-cell therapy: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278154/
  - Result: A 2012 study of this approach treated 8 dogs after CHOP chemotherapy. Median survival was 392 days, compared with 167 days in 12 dogs from earlier records that received CHOP alone.
  - Limit: The comparison was small and used historical controls, not randomly assigned groups. It supports further study of T-cell therapy but does not establish the outcome of today’s commercial protocol.
- ELIAS Cancer Immunotherapy (ECI): https://eliasanimalhealth.com/elias-cancer-immunotherapy/veterinary-resources/
  - Result: In a published pilot study of 14 dogs treated after amputation, median survival was 415 days; 5 dogs lived more than two years. There was no chemotherapy comparison group.
  - Limit: A later randomized study compared ECI with carboplatin, but ELIAS still lists its publication as pending. The pilot alone cannot show that ECI is better than standard chemotherapy.
- Gilvetmab: https://academic.oup.com/jvim/article/40/3/aalag098/8703156
  - Result: In a 2026 study, tumors shrank or disappeared in 12 of 26 dogs with mast cell tumors (46%) and 5 of 25 dogs with melanoma (20%).
  - Limit: There was no comparison group, so the study cannot show whether gilvetmab works better than other treatments or extends survival. Stable disease was counted separately from tumor shrinkage.
- Radioactive iodine (I-131): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15907038/
  - Result: A 2005 review of 65 dogs with thyroid carcinoma reported median survival of 30 months for dogs receiving radioactive iodine alone and 34 months when it was combined with surgery.
  - Limit: These were selected patients reviewed from medical records, not randomly assigned treatment groups. The figures do not predict an individual dog’s survival or prove an advantage over other treatments.
- Toceranib (Palladia): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/vco.12571
  - Result: A review of 42 dogs with thyroid carcinoma included 26 receiving toceranib without previous cancer treatment. In that group, the median time before the cancer progressed was 206 days.
  - Limit: This was a review of medical records without a randomized comparison group. It suggests the drug can control some thyroid cancers but does not show that it is better than surgery or radiation.
- Oncept: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23909996/
  - Result: Published results are mixed. A study comparing records of 45 dogs with locally controlled oral melanoma found no longer survival or time without progression in vaccinated dogs than in unvaccinated dogs.
  - Limit: Other studies report outcomes in vaccinated dogs, but many lack a comparable unvaccinated group. A survival benefit remains uncertain despite USDA licensure.
- Laverdia (verdinexor): https://animaldrugsatfda.fda.gov/adafda/app/search/public/document/downloadFoi/17795
  - Result: In the randomized study supporting full FDA approval, 134 dogs were evaluated for effectiveness. Median time before lymphoma progressed was 37 days with Laverdia and 23 days with placebo.
  - Limit: This measured delay in progression, not overall survival. The comparison was with placebo, not CHOP, so it does not show that Laverdia provides the same benefit as standard combination chemotherapy.
- Tanovea (rabacfosadine): https://animaldrugsatfda.fda.gov/adafda/app/search/public/document/downloadFoi/11083
  - Result: In a randomized placebo-controlled study, lymphoma shrank or became undetectable in 82 of 112 dogs receiving Tanovea (73%), including 57 complete responses. The placebo response rate was 6%.
  - Limit: A complete response means no detectable cancer at assessment, not a cure. The study did not compare Tanovea directly with CHOP chemotherapy.
- CanCan K9-LiquiDX: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/vco.70050
  - Result: A conference report described use in 55 dogs at one clinic. Among 45 completed tests, 37 identified potential drug options. Seven dogs had repeat blood testing; in two soft tissue sarcoma cases, the blood signal disappeared after treatment.
  - Limit: These are early clinical observations. Finding a possible drug target is not proof that the drug will help, and this report does not establish how reliably the test detects relapse before scans.
- Vidium SearchLight DNA: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36862546/
  - Result: In a study of 69 dogs with unclear cancer diagnoses, genomic testing helped clarify the diagnosis in 37 cases (54%). It provided treatment or prognostic information in another 22 cases.
  - Limit: This shows that the test can add useful information in difficult cases. It does not by itself show that following a suggested drug improves survival.
- VetOmics Canine CGP: https://vetomicsanimalhealth.com/publication/
  - Result: The company reports laboratory validation of the test’s ability to detect DNA changes. That checks the measurement, rather than whether treatment chosen from the report helps dogs live longer.
  - Limit: Its publication list includes studies of other genomic panels. Their results cannot automatically be applied to Canine CGP, and each suggested treatment still needs its own supporting evidence.
- FidoCure genomic guidance: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-89862-9
  - Result: A 2025 study analyzed records of 508 dogs with splenic hemangiosarcoma. Targeted-drug treatment was associated with longer survival after surgery, and some DNA changes were linked with treatment outcomes.
  - Limit: Dogs were not randomly assigned to treatments, so differences between patients may explain part of the result. It does not prove that testing itself caused the benefit or that the findings apply to every cancer.

CanCan abstract contains an internal percentage/count inconsistency for no-mutation cases; those figures were not used. Its potential-drug-option yield is not diagnostic accuracy or a demonstrated treatment response. Aurelius card describes the foundational approach study, not a validation of the current commercial protocol. ELIAS randomized publication remains pending on its current veterinary-resources page; no unpublished comparative survival number was invented.
